# A.6.2 — Security responsibility in employment and engagement relationships

## Purpose

Security responsibility in employment and engagement relationships ensures that people know their security-relevant duties, boundaries, and escalation paths before and during their activity. Responsibility is not only mentioned in contracts, but translated into role, everyday work, leadership, and evidence.

## Control objective in repository language

The organisation ensures that employees and external participants receive clear security responsibility for their role, that this is confirmed, remains visible in onboarding and work routines, and is updated when changes occur.

## Typical risks

- If security responsibility only appears in general clauses, people do not know in everyday work what they must decide, report, or refrain from doing.
- If external staff are not included, gaps arise in data handling, reporting paths, and access use.
- If managers do not activate responsibility, conflicting objectives between delivery pressure and security remain unresolved.
- If role changes are not followed up, obligations and access no longer match the task.
- If violations or uncertainties are not escalated, misconduct remains invisible and repeats.

## Triggers

- entry, engagement, role change, or assumption of special responsibility.
- change in policy, data class, reporting path, tooling, or work model.
- new provider role, subcontractor, or external access.
- security event, rule violation, audit finding, or lessons learned.
- annual review of roles, commitments, and awareness content.
- management decision on security culture, responsibilities, or resources.

## Roles and responsibilities

- **HR / People function:** anchors commitments in onboarding, role changes, and people processes.
- **Manager / Client:** explains role-specific expectations, ensures everyday transfer, and escalates conflicts.
- **ISMS Owner / Security role:** defines minimum content, reporting paths, stop points, and review logic.
- **Legal / Data protection:** reviews contract, employee, data protection, and co-determination questions.
- **Procurement / Supplier management:** transfers requirements to external roles and contracts.
- **Employees / external participants:** confirm awareness, act within the rules, and report uncertainties.
- **Management:** decides on resources, cultural topics, and irresolvable conflicting objectives.

## Implementation

### Minimum start

Goal: Every person in scope knows their basic security responsibility.

1. Basic duties are described in understandable language: data handling, access use, reporting path, confidentiality, secure work equipment, stop points.
2. Onboarding or engagement includes a documented confirmation.
3. Critical roles receive a short additional responsibility with concrete examples.
4. External staff receive equivalent orientation before access or activity begins.
5. Role changes trigger an update of responsibility.

Minimum evidence:

- responsibility or commitment module,
- onboarding/engagement evidence,
- role list for additional responsibility,
- confirmed reporting path,
- update on role change.

### Solid practice

Goal: Security responsibility is operated and tracked by role.

1. Role profiles connect tasks, data classes, access, security decisions, and reporting duties in everyday work.
2. Managers confirm that expectations are understood and workable in the team.
3. Supplier and external roles are reflected in engagement documents and onboarding.
4. Changes to rules, tools, or risks are communicated in a target-group-specific way.
5. Violations, uncertainties, and incidents feed into training, leadership, and process improvement.
6. Sample checks review whether confirmations, role profiles, and access reality match.

Strong evidence:

- role-based responsibility matrix,
- confirmed onboarding and role-change evidence,
- communication evidence for changed rules,
- supplier/external onboarding,
- sample review,
- actions from violations or incidents.

### Advanced practice

Goal: Security responsibility is part of leadership, supplier, and governance routines.

1. Role responsibility is connected with IAM, awareness, supplier management, and performance/leadership routines, where legally clarified.
2. Critical decision roles practise conflicting objectives in scenarios or tabletop formats.
3. Responsibilities are assessed in management reviews based on findings, reporting quality, and recurring error patterns.
4. External roles are regularly reviewed through contract, onboarding, and evidence logic.
5. Metrics show coverage of critical roles, open confirmations, role changes without update, and recurring misunderstandings.

## Routine flow

1. **Role arises or changes:** employment, engagement, role change, or new task.
2. **Derive responsibility:** determine data, access, decisions, reporting paths, and stop points.
3. **Communicate:** understandable, close to the role, and before relevant activity.
4. **Confirm:** document acknowledgement, briefing, or contract/engagement module.
5. **Translate into work:** manager or client clarifies practical expectations.
6. **Follow up changes:** update new rules, tools, risks, or roles.
7. **Review:** evaluate sample checks, incidents, and violations for gaps in responsibility.
8. **Escalate:** route conflicting objectives, non-implementation, or unclear responsibility to management.

## Decisions

- Which basic responsibility applies to all people in scope?
- Which roles need additional or stricter responsibility?
- How are external participants included and evidenced?
- Who may approve access when responsibility is unclear or not accepted?
- Which violations or repeated errors lead to manager or management involvement?
- Which personal evaluations are permissible and necessary?

## Evidence

### Strong evidence

- current responsibility matrix per role cluster,
- confirmed commitment or onboarding evidence,
- role-specific briefings,
- evidence for external people and providers,
- review of role changes,
- improvement actions from incidents, rule violations, or audits.

### Weak evidence

- general contract clause without everyday transfer,
- policy link without confirmation or target group,
- training rate without reference to security responsibility,
- outdated role description,
- blanket assurance from the provider without person or role reference.

### Evidence gaps

- external staff without commitment evidence,
- critical roles without additional responsibility,
- role changes without updated responsibility,
- reporting path unknown or untested,
- repeated violations without process or leadership action.

## Effectiveness review

Review questions:

- Can people in critical roles name their security responsibility in practical terms?
- Are responsibility, access, and role consistent?
- Are external participants included before access?
- Are changes to rules communicated in a target-group-specific way?
- Do violations or incidents lead to adjustments to responsibility, training, or process?
- Are data protection/legal questions clarified for personal tracking?

Possible metrics:

- coverage of confirmed responsibility in scope,
- critical roles with additional briefing,
- role changes with updated responsibility,
- open confirmations,
- external people without evidence,
- recurring violations per role cluster.

## BSIG/NIS2 connection point

Clear assignment of security responsibility is a connection point for NIS2-oriented governance, security culture, training, access protection, incident capability, and supply-chain control. The specific relevance should be assessed for the organisation in the requirements register and management review.

This artefact does not replace legal, employment-law, or data-protection review.

## Boundaries

- No legal or data protection advice on contract, employment, or co-determination questions.
- No statement that confirmation alone proves effective security.
- No personal performance or behaviour monitoring without suitable clarification.
- No certification or conformity commitment.
- No ISO 27002 text or real personal/organisational data.

## Handoffs

- **HR handoff:** onboarding, role changes, commitments, manager communication.
- **Legal/Data protection handoff:** contract modules, employee data, personal tracking, co-determination.
- **Access handoff:** access may only match clarified role and responsibility.
- **Supplier handoff:** external people, subcontractors, engagement boundaries, and evidence.
- **Incident/Disciplinary handoff:** suspected violation, repetition, damage potential, or investigation need.
- **Management handoff:** cultural problems, conflicting objectives, recurring violations, or unclear acceptance of responsibility.

## Typical mistakes

- Responsibility is formulated only legally and not explained operationally.
- All roles receive the same notices although risks differ strongly.
- External people receive access before responsibility is clarified.
- Managers delegate security responsibility completely to IT or ISMS.
- Changes to tools or rules are not communicated to affected target groups.
- Violations are handled in isolation without improving the responsibility routine.

## Fictional mini example

A fictional SaaS provider introduces a new role profile for support employees who can view customer data. HR adds data protection and security responsibility to onboarding, the Support Lead explains typical stop points, and the ISMS Owner updates the reporting path. An external support provider receives the same role module before access. After a misunderstanding during data exports, the briefing is supplemented with a concrete example.

Evidence:

- support role profile,
- confirmed briefing,
- updated reporting path,
- evidence for external support roles,
- action from the data export finding.
