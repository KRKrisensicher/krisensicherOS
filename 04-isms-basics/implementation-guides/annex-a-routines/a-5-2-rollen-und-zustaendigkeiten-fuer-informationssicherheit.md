# A.5.2 — Roles and responsibilities for information security

## Purpose

Roles and responsibilities ensure that information security does not fall between management, IT, business units and service providers. Every relevant security task needs an accountable role, clear decision rights, deputies and a path for escalation.

## Control objective in repository language

The organization operates a traceable role routine for information security. For central security tasks it is clear who is accountable, who contributes, who reviews, who decides and when management or specialist roles are involved.

## Typical risks

- If roles are only named on paper, measures, reviews and exceptions remain unhandled.
- If owners have no decision authority, risks are accepted informally.
- If IT is considered solely responsible, business decisions on protection needs, data and processes are missing.
- If deputies are missing, incidents, reviews or approvals stall during absences.
- If service provider responsibilities are unclear, gaps arise between contract, operations and evidence.
- If several roles are “somehow” responsible for the same task, no one is accountable.

## Triggers

- establishment or change of the ISMS, new scope or new security routine.
- new systems, processes, data classes, locations or suppliers.
- role change, reorganization, onboarding or departure of relevant responsible persons.
- security event, audit finding, overdue measure or escalation.
- management decision on risk, budget, priority or resource conflict.
- regular review of roles and responsibilities.

## Roles and responsibilities

- **Management:** confirms the responsibility model, decision rights, escalation paths and resources.
- **ISMS owner / security role:** maintains role matrix, minimum responsibilities, review rhythm and evidence logic.
- **Risk owner:** is accountable for concrete risks, measures, acceptances and escalations.
- **Asset owner / information owner:** makes business decisions on protection needs, use and access.
- **Process owner:** integrates security requirements into workflows and controls.
- **IT/platform owner:** is accountable for technical implementation, operational evidence and technical risks in their area.
- **HR / people function:** supports role changes, job profiles, onboarding and deputies.
- **Service provider/vendor owner:** steers external responsibilities, evidence and escalation contacts.

## Implementation

### Minimum start

Objective: The most important security tasks have a named owner and escalation point.

1. The organization lists central security routines: risk, access, incident, suppliers, awareness, vulnerabilities, backup, BCM, policies.
2. A primary owner is named for each routine.
3. Deputies and escalation points are defined at least for critical routines.
4. Owners confirm their area of responsibility and open decision boundaries.
5. Unclear or unfilled roles are documented as a management topic.
6. A review date checks currency after reorganization or role changes.

Minimum evidence:

- roles/responsibilities matrix,
- owner confirmation or management approval,
- list of open role gaps,
- escalation path,
- review date.

### Solid practice

Objective: Roles are connected with tasks, decisions, evidence and deputies.

1. For central routines, the following is described: accountable, contributing, reviewing, deciding, informed.
2. Decision rights are clarified: risk acceptance, exception, budget, priority, external communication.
3. Roles are referenced in onboarding, job profiles, process descriptions or operational documentation.
4. Service provider roles are paired with internal owners so responsibility does not disappear through outsourcing.
5. Overdue measures, unclear owners or recurring escalations flow into management review.
6. Role changes are connected with HR, access and knowledge transfer routines.

### Advanced practice

Objective: Responsibilities are operated as an active governance system.

1. Role matrix, asset inventory, risk register, measures log and evidence pack are connected.
2. Workflows prevent tickets, risks or findings without an owner.
3. Deputies, competence requirements and capacity risks are reviewed regularly.
4. Management sees role bottlenecks, single points of failure and conflicts that cannot be decided operationally.
5. Exercises or tabletop scenarios test whether roles work in an incident or crisis.
6. Roles are adjusted early during organizational development and new services.

## Routine flow

1. **Recognize task or change:** new routine, new asset, finding, incident, role change or reorganization.
2. **Determine responsibility need:** Which decision, implementation, review or escalation is needed?
3. **Assign owner:** define primary role, deputy and involved roles.
4. **Clarify decision rights:** What may the role decide independently, what must be escalated?
5. **Transfer into registers:** update role matrix, risk register, process documentation or ticketing.
6. **Communicate:** inform affected people and interfaces.
7. **Review effectiveness:** evaluate open measures, delays, findings and escalations.
8. **Adjust:** follow up roles, capacity, training or management decision.

## Decisions

- Which security tasks are critical enough for explicit owners and deputies?
- Who may accept risks, exceptions and deadline extensions?
- Which responsibilities remain internal, even when service providers take on operational tasks?
- Which roles need specific enablement or management backing?
- When does a role gap become a management topic?
- How are responsibilities kept current during reorganizations?

## Evidence

### Strong evidence

- current roles and responsibilities matrix with scope,
- owner confirmations for critical routines or assets,
- documented deputies and escalation paths,
- measures, risk or finding lists with clear owners,
- management decisions on role gaps or resources,
- evidence that role changes were transferred into access and routines.

### Weak evidence

- organization chart without security tasks,
- policy with role titles but without persons or functions,
- old RACI table without review date,
- tickets with team names instead of accountable owner,
- service provider contract without internal counterpart.

### Evidence gaps

- risks or measures without owner,
- no deputy for critical security roles,
- unclear authority for risk acceptance,
- role change without handover,
- external responsibilities without internal steering,
- recurring findings because “no one is responsible”.

## Effectiveness review

Review questions:

- Does every critical security routine have an owner and a deputy?
- Can owners explain their decisions, boundaries and escalation paths?
- Are roles updated during onboarding, changes, departures and reorganization?
- Do risks, measures and findings have accountable persons or functions?
- Are service provider tasks steered internally?
- Does management recognize capacity and competence gaps?

Possible metrics:

- share of critical routines with owner and deputy,
- open measures without owner,
- overdue reviews due to missing responsibility,
- role gaps in management review,
- findings with root cause “unclear responsibility”,
- time until owner assignment for new findings.

## BSIG/NIS2 connection point

Clear roles and responsibilities are connectable to NIS2-oriented governance, management oversight, risk management, incident capability and evidence handling. For affected organizations, it should be assessed in the requirements register which roles, decision rights and evidence are relevant.

This artifact does not make a binding statement on legal applicability or on the personal responsibility of individual persons.

## Boundaries

- No legal advice on duties of corporate bodies or personal liability.
- No data protection advice on roles in data protection law.
- No certification or conformity assurance.
- No organizational decision by templates or aids.
- No effective control when roles are named without time, competence or decision rights.

## Handoffs

- **Management handoff:** role gaps, capacity problems, unclear decision rights, unacceptable single points of failure.
- **HR handoff:** role changes, job profiles, onboarding, departure, deputies and enablement.
- **Risk handoff:** risks or measures without owner, open risk acceptance.
- **Vendor handoff:** external tasks, service provider contacts, evidence and escalation paths.
- **Incident/BCM handoff:** roles for reporting paths, crisis communication, restart and decisions in an event.
- **Audit/evidence handoff:** evidence on roles, decisions, handovers and review status.

## Typical mistakes

- Roles are named but not filled with tasks or decisions.
- One central security role is made responsible for everything.
- Business units do not provide owners for information or processes.
- Deputies exist only informally.
- Service providers are understood as a substitute for responsibility.
- The role matrix is not updated after reorganizations.
- Management does not receive role gaps as a decision question.

## Fictional mini example

A fictional software provider notices that vulnerability tickets often remain undecided for weeks. The ISMS owner creates a role matrix: product owners assess business risk, platform owners handle technical findings, and management decides on overdue critical exceptions. HR adds the security roles to the onboarding of new managers.

Evidence:

- role matrix for the vulnerability routine,
- owner confirmation from product and platform owners,
- escalation rule for critical exceptions,
- updated onboarding component,
- review note after three months with fewer ownerless tickets.
