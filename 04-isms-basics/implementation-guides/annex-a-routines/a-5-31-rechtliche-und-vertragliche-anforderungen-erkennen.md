# A.5.31 — Identifying legal and contractual requirements

## Purpose

Identifying legal and contractual requirements ensures that information-security-relevant obligations, commitments, and restrictions are not discovered by chance. The routine creates a manageable requirements register and clear handoffs to Legal, Data Protection, Procurement, Management, and business functions.

The core is not legal advice by the ISMS, but traceable governance: Which requirements could be relevant, who assesses them professionally or legally, how are they translated into controls and evidence, and when are changes detected?

## Control objective in repository language

The organization operates a routine to identify, assign, keep current, and transfer information-security-relevant legal, regulatory, contractual, and self-defined requirements into implementable measures, responsibilities, and evidence. Binding interpretation remains with qualified human roles.

## Typical risks

- If requirements are not recorded systematically, obligations, customer promises, or contractual commitments remain unnoticed.
- If Legal, Data Protection, and the ISMS work separately, gaps arise between assessment, implementation, and evidence.
- If contracts contain security requirements but are not handed over to operations or supplier management, commitments remain unmet or unverifiable.
- If new products, countries, sectors, or customer groups do not act as triggers, the requirements picture becomes outdated.
- If requirements are uncritically marked as “fulfilled”, pseudo-conformity arises.
- If evidence is missing, the organization cannot robustly explain its own decisions, obligations, or commitments.

## Triggers

- new customer, contract, tender, supplier, market, country, product, or service.
- change in laws, regulation, customer requirements, sector requirements, or internal policies.
- audit, customer questionnaire, due diligence request, or management question.
- new or changed data processing, cloud, outsourcing, or service provider process.
- security incident, crisis, or suspected reporting obligation.
- review of the requirements register, risk register, or management review.
- finding that a control routine has no clear requirement source or evidence logic.

## Roles and responsibilities

- **ISMS owner / Security governance:** maintains the requirements register and links requirements with controls, risks, and evidence.
- **Legal:** assesses legal requirements, contractual interpretation, external communication, and binding statements.
- **Data Protection:** assesses data-protection requirements, personal data, and evidence obligations within its own mandate.
- **Procurement / Vendor Management:** transfers requirements into supplier governance, contracts, and reviews.
- **Sales / Account / Business function:** introduces customer requirements, tenders, and commitments early.
- **Control Owner / Service Owner:** implements assigned requirements in routines, technical measures, and evidence.
- **Management:** decides conflicts of objectives, resources, risk acceptance, and strategic commitments.

## Implementation

### Minimum start

Goal: make relevant requirements visible and prevent them from being lost in individual mailboxes.

1. Create a simple requirements register: source, short description, owner, assessment handoff, affected scope, status, review date.
2. Collect sources: contracts, customer requirements, internal policies, regulatory notes, data-protection registers, supplier requirements.
3. Clarify for each requirement who assesses it in a binding way: Legal, Data Protection, Management, business function, or ISMS.
4. Link requirements with controls, risks, actions, or evidence.
5. Make open assessments and conflicts of objectives visible in management review.
6. At least every six months, check whether new contracts, services, or changes have been included.

Minimum evidence:

- requirements register,
- assessment or handoff note,
- assignment to control, risk, or action,
- decision on open requirements,
- review note with changes.

### Solid practice

Goal: requirements are regularly identified, assessed, implemented, and tracked.

1. Triggers from Procurement, Sales, Legal, Data Protection, product development, supplier management, and the ISMS are defined.
2. Requirements are classified by origin, binding nature, scope, risk, and evidence need.
3. New contracts and customer requirements include a security/legal/data-protection check before commitment.
4. Control Owners receive implementable requirements with a clear evidence need, not only legal text passages.
5. Changes lead to gap analysis, actions, risk decision, or conscious rejection.
6. The register is connected with risk analysis, statement of applicability, supplier register, data-protection records, and management review.

### Advanced practice

Goal: requirements management becomes part of product, contract, and governance decisions.

1. New services, markets, customer segments, and suppliers trigger an early requirements review.
2. Contract clauses, customer requirements, and regulatory topics are translated into reusable control and evidence patterns.
3. Workflows connect legal review, data-protection review, security review, procurement, and management decision.
4. Changes are versioned, provided with impact analysis, and assigned to the affected owners.
5. Management sees requirements risks: unassessed requirements, commitments that cannot be fulfilled, resource gaps, critical contract deviations.
6. External statements on conformity, certification, or fulfilment of obligations are approved by humans before publication.

## Routine flow

1. **Requirement is identified:** from a contract, legal change, customer question, audit, supplier, product idea, or internal policy.
2. **Record the source:** capture document, origin, date, affected scope, and initial description.
3. **Set assessment handoff:** involve Legal, Data Protection, business function, Procurement, or Management for binding classification.
4. **Translate ISMS relevance:** identify affected controls, risks, roles, evidence, and processes.
5. **Plan implementation:** define action, owner, deadline, evidence, and open decision.
6. **Decide:** clarify commitment, rejection, exception, contract change, risk acceptance, or resource allocation.
7. **Track:** update status, evidence, and review date in the register.
8. **Review changes:** mirror new requirements, changed contracts, or new services against register and controls.

## Decisions

- Which sources count as inputs for the requirements register?
- Who may make a binding assessment of whether a requirement is applicable, contractually relevant, or to be implemented?
- Which requirements are translated into controls, which into contracts, risks, or management decisions?
- Which customer or sales commitments may not be made without security/legal review?
- How are conflicting requirements or commitments that cannot be fulfilled handled?
- Which evidence is internally sufficient without claiming conformity?
- When does a change need management approval?

## Evidence

### Strong evidence

- current requirements register with owners, sources, scope, and status,
- Legal/Data Protection/business-function assessments or handoff notes,
- assignment to controls, risks, actions, and evidence,
- contract or customer requirement review before commitment,
- gap analysis and action plan for new requirements,
- management decision on conflicts of objectives, resources, or accepted deviations,
- review history of the register.

### Weak evidence

- unsorted contract repository without security assignment,
- general statement “Legal checks this” without handoff or result,
- customer questionnaire without feedback loop to real controls,
- old requirements list without owner or review date,
- blanket conformity claim without evidence and approval logic.

### Evidence gaps

- no overview of relevant contracts or commitments,
- new services without requirements review,
- no connection between requirements and operational controls,
- unassessed legal or data-protection questions,
- customer requirements are answered but not implemented or tracked,
- external statements without documented approval.

## Effectiveness review

Review questions:

- Is there a current register of information-security-relevant requirements?
- Are sources, owners, assessment handoffs, and review dates traceable?
- Are new contracts, customer requirements, and services recognized as triggers?
- Are requirements connected with controls, risks, actions, and evidence?
- Are open assessments and commitments that cannot be fulfilled escalated?
- Are legal and data-protection assessments not replaced by ISMS assumptions?

Possible metrics:

- open unassessed requirements,
- requirements without owner or review date,
- customer/contract requirements checked before commitment,
- actions from new requirements open/overdue,
- external statements without a documented approval process,
- deviations between commitment and available evidence.

## BSIG/NIS2 connection point

This routine is connectable to BSIG/NIS2-oriented governance, risk management, evidence capability, supply-chain governance, incident and continuity requirements, and management responsibility. The specific connection should be maintained in the requirements register in an organization-specific way.

This artifact does not replace legal advice, data-protection advice, or a binding assessment of applicability. It helps make assessment and implementation work visible without claiming conformity.

## Boundaries

- This artifact does not provide legal interpretation or contractual advice.
- It does not confirm fulfilment of legal obligations, certification readiness, or conformity.
- It does not replace Legal, Data Protection, Procurement, or qualified external advice.
- It must not be used as approval for public claims or customer promises.
- Public examples do not contain real contracts, customers, persons, or confidential requirements.

## Handoffs

- **Legal handoff:** legal applicability, contractual interpretation, external commitments, communication, dispute or liability questions.
- **Data Protection handoff:** personal data, processing activities, data subject rights, data-protection incidents, or commissioned processing.
- **Procurement/Vendor handoff:** supplier requirements, contract clauses, evidence, subcontractors, and reviews.
- **Sales/Customer handoff:** tenders, questionnaires, security commitments, deviations, and approvals before response.
- **Control Owner handoff:** implementation in processes, technical measures, training, reviews, and evidence.
- **Management handoff:** requirements that cannot be fulfilled, strategic commitments, resources, risk acceptance, or rejection.
- **Audit/Evidence handoff:** register, assessments, implementation evidence, and review history.

## Typical mistakes

- The ISMS maintains requirements as a list, but nobody assesses them in a binding way.
- Sales or project teams make security commitments before implementation and evidence are clarified.
- Contractual requirements are not passed to Control Owners.
- Legal and data-protection questions are “included” in the security team instead of being handed over.
- Requirements are marked as fulfilled although only a policy exists.
- Changes in services or suppliers do not trigger a register review.
- External conformity statements are used without management, legal, or evidence approval.

## Fictional mini example

A fictional SaaS provider receives a requirement in a tender to provide specific security evidence for operations. The Account Owner forwards the requirement to ISMS, Legal, and Data Protection before submitting the offer. The ISMS maps the evidence to existing controls, Legal reviews the wording of the commitment, and the Service Owner marks two pieces of evidence as not yet robust. Management decides to limit the commitment and start an action to improve evidence.

Evidence:

- register entry for the tender requirement,
- Legal/Data Protection handoff,
- mapping to controls and evidence,
- documented evidence gap,
- management decision on the limited commitment,
- action to improve evidence.
