# A.6.3 — Awareness, training, and enablement

## Purpose

Awareness, training, and enablement ensure that people not only know security requirements, but become capable of acting in their role. The goal is not annual mandatory click-through training, but a learning and enablement process that supports real tasks, risks, reporting paths, and decisions.

## Control objective in repository language

The organisation operates a role-based learning routine for information security. Employees, managers, administrators, developers, business units, and external participants receive suitable orientation, practise relevant situations, and know when they must escalate or seek help.

## Typical risks

- If people do not know reporting paths, security events remain untreated for too long.
- If training remains generic, critical roles do not understand their concrete duties.
- If managers cannot classify security decisions, conflicting objectives remain unresolved.
- If new employees are trained too late, initial risks arise in access, data handling, and tool use.
- If developers, administrators, or business units receive no role-based enablement, avoidable misconfigurations, insecure processes, or data leaks arise.

## Triggers

- entry of new employees or external staff.
- role change, assumption of leadership, admin, development, or process responsibility.
- new tool, new process, new data class, or new threat situation.
- security event, near miss, phishing wave, or audit finding.
- change in policies, reporting paths, or work models.
- annual or semi-annual review of the awareness and training plan.
- management decision on risk culture, resources, or target-group prioritisation.

## Roles and responsibilities

- **ISMS Owner / Security role:** defines learning objectives, risk reference, minimum content, and review logic.
- **HR / People function:** integrates security enablement into onboarding, role changes, and exit.
- **Managers:** ensure participation, everyday transfer, and escalation capability.
- **Business and Process Owners:** provide role-specific scenarios and review practical usefulness.
- **IT/Security team:** provides current threat and incident insights.
- **Data protection / Legal:** reviews personal training evaluations, behaviour-monitoring risks, and mandatory communication.
- **Management:** decides resources, prioritisation, and cultural topics.

## Implementation

### Minimum start

Goal: Every person in scope knows basic rules, reporting path, and their own security role.

1. A compact onboarding module explains data classes, reporting path, phishing, password/MFA basics, secure tool use, and stop points.
2. Critical roles receive a short additional briefing: managers, admins, developers, procurement, HR, or business units with sensitive data.
3. Participation is documented traceably.
4. A reporting path is communicated visibly and tested regularly.
5. At least once a year, contents are reviewed for fit with the risk situation.

Minimum evidence:

- training plan or learning matrix,
- onboarding evidence,
- target group list,
- communication evidence for the reporting path,
- review note with adjustments.

### Solid practice

Goal: Learning is role-based, risk-oriented, and repeatable.

1. Target groups are clustered by risk and responsibility.
2. Learning objectives are formulated per target group: recognise, decide, report, implement securely.
3. Incidents, audits, vulnerabilities, and new ways of working feed into content.
4. Managers receive decision and escalation scenarios.
5. Exercises or short simulations check whether reporting paths work.
6. Non-participation or critical gaps are tracked.
7. Results are addressed in ISMS review and, where needed, in management review.

Strong evidence:

- role-based learning matrix,
- training or exercise materials in the organisation’s own language,
- participation and follow-up list,
- results from exercises or reporting-path tests,
- lessons learned from incidents,
- management decision on resources or target groups.

### Advanced practice

Goal: Security enablement becomes part of the work culture and measurably improves behaviour and decision capability.

1. Learning content is updated modularly and event-based.
2. High-risk groups receive practical scenarios, tabletop exercises, or peer reviews.
3. Awareness is connected with incident response, BCMS, secure development, supplier management, and data protection handoffs.
4. Metrics measure not only participation, but response capability: reporting times, quality of reports, exercise results, repeated errors.
5. Managers use security learning objectives in team routines and retrospectives.
6. Data protection and employee representation are involved when training evaluations become personal or behaviour-related.

## Routine flow

1. **Risk and target-group review:** Which roles have which security decisions or user-error risks?
2. **Define learning objectives:** What must people be able to recognise, do, avoid, or escalate?
3. **Create or update content:** short, close to the role, with examples and reporting path.
4. **Deliver:** onboarding, mandatory module, workshop, exercise, team impulse, or role briefing.
5. **Capture evidence:** participation, target group, date, content, open follow-up points.
6. **Review effectiveness:** exercise, quiz, incident evaluation, reporting-path test, or qualitative feedback.
7. **Improve:** adapt content, frequency, target groups, and communication channels.
8. **Escalate:** route resource gaps, high non-participation, or critical role gaps to management review.

## Decisions

- Which target groups need more than basic awareness?
- Which content is mandatory, and which is role- or risk-based?
- How quickly must new people be trained after entry?
- Which training data may be evaluated personally?
- Which non-participation or test results trigger escalation?
- Which topics belong in management or leadership formats?

## Evidence

### Strong evidence

- learning matrix with roles and risks,
- onboarding and refresher training evidence,
- evidence of role-specific briefings,
- results from exercises, phishing simulations, or reporting-path tests,
- actions from lessons learned,
- management decision for resource or cultural problems.

### Weak evidence

- one-time slide repository without target group,
- participation rate without reference to critical roles,
- generic e-learning without reporting path or practical relevance,
- outdated policy communication without evidence of understanding,
- awareness campaign without review or improvement.

### Evidence gaps

- new people without timely onboarding,
- external staff not captured,
- admins, developers, or managers without additional enablement,
- no evidence that reporting paths are known or tested,
- personal training evaluation without data protection clarification.

## Effectiveness review

Review questions:

- Do new people in scope know how to report security events?
- Is there role-based content for critical tasks?
- Have trainings led to recognisable improvements?
- Are incidents and audit findings translated into learning content?
- Are non-participation and critical gaps tracked?
- Are data protection and employee representation topics clarified for evaluations?

Possible metrics:

- onboarding completion within defined deadline,
- coverage of critical roles,
- overdue refresher training rate,
- reporting-path test rate,
- quality and speed of security reports,
- open actions from lessons learned.

## BSIG/NIS2 connection point

Awareness and enablement are a connection point for NIS2-oriented requirements on cyber hygiene, training, governance, incident capability, and management responsibility. For affected organisations, the requirements register should assess which target groups, evidence, and management decisions are required.

This artefact makes no legal statement on applicability and does not replace review of employment-law, data-protection, or co-determination-relevant questions.

## Boundaries

- No legal or data protection advice.
- No statement that participation rates alone prove security or conformity.
- No personal performance or behaviour monitoring without suitable clarification.
- No ISO 27002 text or certification commitment.
- No real personal or organisational data in public examples.

## Handoffs

- **HR handoff:** onboarding, role changes, mandatory briefings, external staff.
- **Data protection/Legal handoff:** personal training evaluations, phishing simulations, behaviour monitoring, employment-law questions.
- **Incident handoff:** training need from incidents, near misses, or reporting problems.
- **Management handoff:** resources, cultural problems, non-participation of critical roles, repeated violations.
- **Audit/Evidence handoff:** evidence on target groups, delivery, effectiveness, and improvements.
- **BCM/Crisis handoff:** exercises on reporting paths, crisis roles, and decision capability.

## Typical mistakes

- Awareness is understood as annual mandatory training and not as enablement.
- All target groups receive the same content.
- Managers are not prepared for security decisions.
- Reporting paths are communicated but never tested.
- Training success is assessed only by participation rate.
- Training data is evaluated personally without data protection clarification.
- Lessons learned from incidents do not reach training planning.

## Fictional mini example

A fictional service provider introduces a new ticketing system. The onboarding module explains the security reporting path. In addition, support staff receive a 30-minute briefing on suspicious customer attachments and data classification. After a phishing wave, a reporting-path test is performed. The result shows that external staff do not know the reporting channel. HR and the ISMS Owner therefore add it to external onboarding.

Evidence:

- updated learning matrix,
- participation in the support briefing,
- reporting-path test result,
- action to supplement external onboarding,
- review note in the ISMS action log.
